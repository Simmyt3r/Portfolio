Africa Finance📊 Staggering

$1.4 Trillion Moved Through Mobile Money in Sub-Saharan Africa Last Year — A Number So Big It Needs a Full Explanation

A trillion is a million millions. Fourteen hundred billion. To try to visualise it: if you stacked one trillion one-naira coins, the stack would reach from Earth to the Moon and back — 240 times. That is not what we are talking about here. We are talking about $1.4 trillion US dollars — the total value of money transferred through mobile money services across sub-Saharan Africa in 2025, according to the GSMA, the global mobile industry association. Mobile money services — like M-Pesa in Kenya, MTN MoMo in Nigeria, or Airtel Money — allow people to send, receive, save, and spend money using only a basic mobile phone, without needing a traditional bank account. They were invented specifically because hundreds of millions of Africans had phones but no bank accounts. In 2025, $1.4 trillion flowed through these systems. For comparison: the entire Nigerian economy produces about $500 billion in value per year. So in one year, African mobile money moved nearly three times Nigeria's entire economic output. What this tells us: financial infrastructure in Africa is not broken — it is working at a scale most people do not realise. The opportunity for entrepreneurs is not to replace this infrastructure but to build on top of it — creating savings products, insurance, credit, investments, and merchant services that reach people who are already connected to the financial system through their phones.

When a startup needs money to grow, it has two main options. Option 1: Sell a piece of the company to an investor in exchange for cash. This is called equity. If you sell equity (a piece of your company) to raise money early, you lose part of your ownership — forever. If you borrow money instead, you pay it back and keep full ownership of everything you built. Startups are now choosing to protect their ownership. This is only possible because African startups are now generating enough real revenue that banks and development finance institutions trust them enough to lend. That is a sign of a maturing ecosystem — not a struggling one. The important warning from the data: investors who once funded ideas are now funding proven businesses only. If you are a founder, the message is clear: build something that earns first. Fundraising comes after proof.

South Africa's Government Used AI to Write an Official Policy — The AI Made Up Fake References and Nobody Noticed Until It Was Published

Think of it like a student copying their homework from a classmate who made up all the answers. The classmate's answers look neat and convincing, but they are all wrong. Because AI tools are becoming powerful enough that we use them for important things — legal documents, medical research, business plans, school assignments. But AI is only as good as the human reviewing its output. If you use AI without verifying what it tells you, you are handing in the classmate's made-up homework. The rule for every entrepreneur: AI is a tool, not a fact-checker. Always verify before you publish, present, or act on AI-generated information.
